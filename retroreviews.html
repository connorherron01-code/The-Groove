"Fever" by Kylie Minogue

What do we do with disco? This was a question that has been on people's minds since the 80s. After Disco Demolition Night cratered the value of disco, music floundered before New Wave and the Thriller moment showed how R&B and dance songs would move through the rest of the decade. Disco became cool again in the 90s thanks to the 20-year nostalgia cycle and the latest descendants of the 4-on-the-floor pattern figured out what to do with it. House, techno, and all of their cousins were taking the tricks of Disco and moving them into a world of 303s, 808s, and 909s. Daft Punk and the French Touch movement were one standard bearer. And as the 90s turned into the 2000s, Kylie Minogue turned out to be another one.

Australian soap opera star in the 80s turned British teen-pop star, Kylie's first big moment was lifted mostly on the strength of her producers, Stock, Aitken, and Waterman, who gave her tinny, cheap, but electric little songs that moved most off of her energy and enthusiasm. SAW's style was fun while it lasted, and it has given us the genuine Hi-NRG classic "You Spin Me Round (Like A Record)" by Dead Or Alive, as well as Rick Astley's immortal "Never Gonna Give You Up", but it had a shelf life. And Minogue's thin voice didn't seem especially keen to stick around. She held on up until the early 90s, getting a lot of #1 hits in Britain, but eventually, it seemed like the hits dried up, especially after the misunderstood gem Impossible Princess got mocked in the mid-90s. She kept trucking along with Light Years, getting another #1 in "Spinning Around", but she seemed to be winding down for the most part.

That presumption ends with "Can't Get You Outta My Head", a 10/10 pop song that moves like a factory machine line made of glass and crystal. The production is absolutely pristine, a hall of mirrors that gets bigger the more you look at it. Many hooks are claimed to be 'timeless', the kind of thing you're amazed nobody came up with, and I'd like to give that honor to that simple "la la la, la la la-la-la" hook. And Minogue delivers it. Her voice isn't necessarily any stronger than it was before, but she utilizes it more effectively, using a breathier register that seems like it's both putting you in a trance and herself, entranced-the "set me freeeeeeeeeeeee" and the "forever and ever and ever and ever..." parts especially.

The keywords for the whole project are 'pristine' and 'playful'. The keyboards sound clean, precise, and everything moves like a well-oiled machine, but there'rs also joy and fun to be had. Kylie sometimes sounds distant, but she never feels unemotional. "Love At First Sight" takes the thing that brought her to the dancefloor: winning optimism and sunny disposition-and updates it for a world of nu-disco. She sounds ecstatic and in love, which sounds like a no duh thing to praise, but trust me, it makes a world of difference. "Come Into My World" is a inginueish invitation to a special place. Every collaborator is doing their best, from onetime dance star Cathy Dennis to Mud's Rob Davis. The parallel that I want to draw is to Lady Gaga at the end of the 2000s-Gaga made music that was artsy, messy, dark, and campy. Here, Kylie is breezy, light, beautiful and clean. "Can't Get You Outta My Head" is almost mantric in it's constant repitition, the clear descendant of Daft Punk's "Around The World", but transposed from robotic to coquettish.

Fever is a towering achievement of the kind of beautiful fusion that happens when the best of pop and the best of dance come together. It's the grand inheritor of Giorgio Moroder and Donna Summer's electro-disco classic "I Feel Love", which sounded like the future in the late 70s. The retro futures of the past could often times age poorly, and you can argue the album's aesthetic, all pure whites and crystal and glass definitely paved the way for the 'Everything Is An iPod' future-aesthetic of the mid and late 2000s. But nobody can really predict the future. Dance music moved on to darker and grimier textures, informed by the acid house of the early 90s that came up around Kylie's beginning, and the retro pulled from the primitive synthpop that tried to stab you with keyboards. Kylie stands as a testament to the pure power of a crystaline dancefloor, nothing more and nothing less.
